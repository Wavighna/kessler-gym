Autonomous collision avoidance for satellites

An AI that teaches satellites to dodge space junk.

Low Earth orbit has 10,000+ active satellites and tens of thousands of tracked debris pieces. One collision at 10 km/s creates thousands more: Kessler syndrome. Kessler is a physics simulator plus a reinforcement-learning pilot that decides when to fire thrusters, which way, and how to get back into its orbit, using as little fuel as possible.

Under the hood

Environment

ẍ = 3n²x + 2nẏ + aₓ      (radial)
ÿ = −2nẋ + a_y           (along-track)
z̈ = −n²z + a_z           (cross-track)
n = √(μ/r³) = 1.10e-3 rad/s   r = 6,928 km

Propagated with the closed-form CW state-transition matrix Φ(τ), so a 4-hour episode costs microseconds. Debris passes at 2–14 km/s, so each encounter is evaluated in its B-plane: only displacement perpendicular to the relative velocity counts.

Observation and reward

  • 28-dim observation: own position, velocity, fuel and clock, plus the two most urgent conjunctions.
  • Per conjunction: time to TCA, noisy predicted miss vector, 1σ uncertainty (400 m → 25 m as tracking improves), Pc from the Rice distribution, and the physics-informed sensitivity ∂|miss|/∂Δv = Φᵣᵥ(τ)ᵀ m̂.
  • Reward: −30 per keep-out violation, a Gaussian proximity penalty, −2 per m/s of Δv, and a quadratic slot-keeping cost.

The trade-off the agent learns

An early burn is cheap because along-track drift grows as 3·Δv·τ, but the miss estimate is still mostly noise. A late burn sees the truth but needs far more Δv. The agent also has to handle overlapping conjunctions, where dodging one object can move it toward the next, and then spend fuel to get back to its slot.

Policy: 28→32→32→3 tanh MLP (2,083 parameters) that outputs an impulsive Δv of up to 8 cm/s per axis each minute.
